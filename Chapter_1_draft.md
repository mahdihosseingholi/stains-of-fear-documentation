# Chapter 1: The Material That Persists

---

## Introduction: The Image That Cannot Be Contained

A film is made of decisions, and decisions are made about materials. Every shot requires a choice of stock, sensor, emulsion chemistry, light source, focal length, duration of exposure. These choices are technical, but they are also ontological: each one determines what kind of reality the image will carry, what it will register, and what it will suppress. Classical fiction filmmaking has traditionally treated this layer of decision as preparatory, preceding the film in importance and disappearing inside it. The materials of cinema are the medium's infrastructure, not its subject. Bordwell, Staiger, and Thompson describe the classical Hollywood system as one organised around the systematic subordination of all technical elements to the demands of narrative causality (Bordwell et al., 1985). The apparatus is there to serve the story and, ideally, to vanish. Metz called this the imaginary signifier: cinema's fundamental operation is to produce presence while concealing the conditions of its own production (Metz, 1982). The screen offers a world; the projector, the celluloid, the emulsion grain, the chemical processes that fix the image remain invisible behind it.

*Stains of Fear* begins from a different premise. The film was shot digitally, as a scripted fiction. It has a character, a location, a narrative situation. But running through and beneath its digital footage are images produced by a third agent: fungal mold cultivated directly on analogue photographic film stock. The mold grew for months in sealed incubation environments, metabolising the gelatin of the emulsion from within. The resulting transformations were then introduced into the film through three distinct production methods, each involving a different relationship between the biological, the analogue, and the digital. The mold was not a special effect. It was not simulated or represented through colour grading. It was a material process, conducted in time, on physical film stock, whose outcomes were registered indexically and carried forward into the finished work.

The research question this film poses is therefore not primarily about narrative, though narrative is present throughout. It is about what happens to a film's images when the material that carries them has been transformed by a living organism. What kind of image does the mold produce? What does it do to the photographic index? What does digital cinema mean as a frame and container when the material it incorporates is biological, mutable, and unrepeatable? The central claim of this thesis is that the mold-transformed emulsion produces a second indexical event — not a photographic trace of a scene, but a biological trace of an organism's encounter with its own substrate — and that the resulting image carries both indices simultaneously. When this doubled image is then scanned and incorporated into a digital fiction, it produces a condition in which the photographic, the biological, and the computational coexist within a single frame, without any one register absorbing the others. The following chapter develops the theoretical tools required to make this claim precise.

## 1.1 The Apparatus and Its Suppression

The history of cinema is partly a history of the progressive refinement of techniques for making its own material basis disappear. This is not incidental to the medium; it is structural. Walter Benjamin's observation that the most perfect reproduction lacks its original's presence in time and space (Benjamin, 1969) describes a deficit that cinema never acknowledges from within its own fictional frame. The film projector replaces the world, but it does not represent itself as a projector. The emulsion grain, the lens flare, the splice mark, the hair caught in the aperture gate: classical editing theory treats these as failures, interruptions in the smooth transfer of attention from medium to story. Murch's principle of the invisible cut encodes this suppression as craft: a cut is successful when the viewer does not perceive it, when the edit is timed to the rhythms of perception so that the apparatus disappears entirely (Murch, 2001). The ideal film, in this tradition, is one whose medium has become perfectly transparent.

Baudry's critique of the apparatus makes the ideological stakes of this transparency explicit. The concealment of the cinematic apparatus does not simply produce pleasure; it produces a specific subject: centred, Cartesian, positioned at the apparent origin point of a world that appears to emanate from nowhere in particular (Baudry, 1974). The classical Hollywood cinema, as Bordwell, Staiger, and Thompson document in exhaustive detail, developed its system of continuous editing, motivated camera movement, and psychologically coherent character precisely in order to sustain this effect (Bordwell et al., 1985). The material conditions of production were standardised so thoroughly that their traces became invisible, absorbed into the seamlessness of the fictional world. Baudry's framework has been substantially challenged — Carroll, Prince, and Bordwell himself have argued that the apparatus does not determine subject-position as mechanistically as Baudry supposed — but the core observation remains useful: classical cinema is designed so that its material substrate does not interrupt its representational surface.

What this suppression produces is not neutral. Kittler, approaching cinema through its technical substrate rather than its content, argues that the significance of any medium lies not in what passes through it but in what the medium itself does to the signal: its specific material and technological conditions of storage, transmission, and reproduction (Kittler, 1999). Dominant cinema has systematically refused this question by treating its medium as a neutral conduit for story. Parikka extends Kittler's materialist argument into the deep time of media: film stock, he observes, has a geology, a chemistry, a temporal life shaped by the mineral and biological origins of its components, and the aesthetic traditions of cinema have excluded this entire dimension from analysis (Parikka, 2015). The emulsion contains a history that the finished film is designed not to show.

There is a precedent for breaking this design, and it emerges not from dominant cinema but from the avant-garde. Gunning's account of the cinema of attractions describes the tendency in early film, and later in certain avant-garde lineages, toward what he calls exhibitionist confrontation rather than diegetic absorption: the direct address of the apparatus to the viewer, the foregrounding of the image as image rather than as window (Gunning, 1990). Three filmmakers in this lineage bear directly on the problem that *Stains of Fear* addresses, each occupying a different position in the relationship between material surface and recorded image.

Brakhage's *Mothlight* (1963) is the most rigorous early instance of the material substrate becoming the image itself. Brakhage pressed moth wings, flower petals, and blades of grass directly between strips of 16mm film and splicing tape, producing images that the camera never made and that carry no photographic index of any scene (MacDonald, 2001). What *Mothlight* registers is not a representation of organic matter but the physical fact of its presence on the film support: each wing, each petal, is a contact trace, a one-to-one imprint whose relationship to the film strip is indexical in Krauss's sense — causally produced, physically contiguous — but whose referent is the object itself rather than a scene reflected in light. The material is not a medium for something else; it is the event. But the organic matter in *Mothlight* is dead. The moth wings and petals are specimens, fixed at the moment of their attachment to the strip. The biological process that produced them is completed; what remains is their physical trace.

Morrison's *Decasia* (2002) arrives at the same problem from the opposite direction. The film works with archival nitrate footage in the process of chemical decomposition, so that the decay of the emulsion enters the image as a visual force that competes with the photographed content for the viewer's attention. A boxer throws punches against billowing chemical formations that are no longer part of the photographed scene but have become the scene's material rival. What makes *Decasia* structurally significant is that it does not resolve the tension between these two registers. The photographed content remains legible — we can still identify figures, gestures, locations — but the chemical decay has become a second visual presence within the frame, one that the original filmmakers never intended and that the passage of time has produced without human direction. Neither the decay nor the image wins. Both coexist, and the viewer oscillates between reading the content and seeing the surface.

The most direct precedent for the biological transformation of film emulsion as an aesthetic practice is the work of the West German collective Schmelzdahin and its principal member, Jürgen Reble. Active between 1979 and 1989, Schmelzdahin subjected film stock to sustained biological and chemical decomposition. Their method was literal: film reels were buried in gardens, submerged in ponds, or stored in conditions that encouraged bacterial and fungal colonisation of the emulsion. *Stadt in Flammen* (1984) took a Super-8 digest of a Hollywood disaster film and buried it in damp earth for six months. Reble's own account describes the result: the superimposed layers of the colour emulsion had split apart and partly mixed together, producing intense chromatic fields that departed from their original photographic function and laid themselves across the action footage in configurations that Reble compared to stained glass (Reble, 1997). There is no original left, he wrote; it is lost to its copy. What distinguishes Reble's practice from Morrison's archival aesthetics is that the decomposition was not found but cultivated. His theoretical position is explicit: beyond a certain point, one is little more than the spectator of these phenomena. This formulation — the artist as witness to a material process rather than as its author — is the nearest direct precedent to the position *Stains of Fear* occupies.

Karel Doing's phytogram practice, developed from 2015 onward, extends this lineage into a different material register. Doing places leaves soaked in an alkaline solution directly on photographic emulsion and exposes the arrangement to sunlight. The plant's own phenolic compounds reduce the exposed silver halides to metallic silver at the points of contact. Doing's published account frames the resulting images as chemical traces produced by the organism itself rather than by the camera or the human hand (Doing, 2020). Doing explicitly contrasts this with Brakhage's *Mothlight*: where Brakhage collaged dead organic matter between strips of film, the phytogram extends a living biochemical process into the photographic substrate.

Peter Tscherkassky's CinemaScope Trilogy — particularly *Outer Space* (1999) — demonstrates what happens when material manipulation is applied to narrative content, though from a different direction. Tscherkassky works in a darkroom without a camera, contact-printing found 35mm Hollywood genre footage onto unexposed stock using handheld light sources, so that the sprocket holes, the optical soundtrack, the multiple exposures, and the tears in the emulsion become the visual equivalent of the invisible force that attacks the protagonist in the original film. The narrative does not disappear; it persists as a legible residue beneath and within the material intervention. But Tscherkassky's material operations are manual and optical rather than biological. The emulsion is manipulated by human hands in controlled conditions. The organism is absent.

Kambiz Shafeei's practice represents a closer formal precedent. Working with photographic prints rather than film stock, Shafeei has theorised what he calls the dual surface of the photograph: the surface as simultaneously a pictorial space and a material object whose physical substrate can be engaged directly, altered, marked (Shafeei, forthcoming). The photograph, in this account, does not have a surface — it *is* a surface, and that surface carries information about its own material history as much as about the scene it represents. Shafeei's framework makes explicit something that Brakhage and Reble demonstrate practically: that the material of the image and the image of the material are not separate registers but the same thing at different analytical moments. *Stains of Fear* operates within this logic, but inside a fiction.

What distinguishes *Stains of Fear* from all of these precedents is the specific combination it proposes: biological material transformation embedded within a fiction that continues to function as fiction. Brakhage, Morrison, Reble, and Doing work outside narrative; their material operations are the film. Tscherkassky works with narrative content but through manual optical manipulation rather than biological process. Shafeei works with the still photograph rather than the moving image. The question this project poses is structurally distinct: what happens when a living organism transforms the material substrate of an image, and that transformed image is then incorporated into a scripted fiction that does not abandon its narrative function? The apparatus does not absorb the material event; the material event does not dissolve the apparatus. The two coexist, and the film must find a way to hold both simultaneously. This is the structural problem the thesis addresses.

## 1.2 The Photographic Index and Its Material Substrate

To understand what the mold does to a film emulsion, it is necessary to understand what the emulsion already is before any biological encounter. A photographic film stock is not a passive support waiting to receive an image. It is a chemically complex, temporally active material: a system held at a particular threshold of readiness. Simondon's account of physical individuation offers the conceptual vocabulary for this condition. A metastable system, in Simondon's terms, is one that contains more potential energy than it can stably carry: it is held above its equilibrium state, susceptible to transformation, poised at a threshold from which a phase shift can be triggered by a relatively small input (Simondon, 2020). An unexposed emulsion is precisely this. Its silver halide crystals are pre-formed, sensitised, structured in anticipation of light. Exposure does not simply record an external event; it triggers an individuation, a phase transition through which the metastable system moves into a new material state. The photograph is the trace of that transition.

The significance of Simondon's framework here extends beyond a technical description of photochemistry. What individuation names is the ontological priority of process over entity: the emulsion does not have properties that are then acted upon by light; it becomes those properties through the encounter with light. The crystal structure, the halide composition, the gelatin density — these are not stable attributes of a pre-existing thing but the conditions of a system poised at a phase boundary. Hansen's subsequent application of Simondon to media theory, particularly his concept of the system-environment hybrid, in which individuation is understood not as an event internal to a single entity but as a process distributed across a coupled system and its milieu (Hansen, 2012; 2015), will become directly relevant when the mold enters the emulsion and the system-environment boundary collapses. The organism does not act upon a stable material; it participates in a system that was already constitutively open to transformation.

This reframing changes the ontological status of the photographic index. Krauss, in her influential account of photography's indexical nature, defines the photograph as a physical imprint transferred by light reflections onto a sensitive surface: a trace causally connected to its referent in the manner of a footprint or a shadow, rather than a representation that resembles it (Krauss, 1977). The index in this account is what Peirce would call a sign whose connection to its object is existential rather than conventional. But what the index registers is not simply the scene. It registers the scene mediated by the specific material state of the emulsion at the moment of exposure: its chemistry, its age, its humidity history, its batch characteristics, the temperature of the developer, the duration of the fix. The index carries the world, but it carries it through a specific material substrate, and that substrate has its own history, its own properties, its own capacity for transformation.

Parikka makes this latency explicit. Writing on the material constitution of photographic media, he observes that the history of photography is as much a history of chemicals as of inventors: "For photochemical artists, getting their hands dirty with gelatin and silver nitrates, this is part of the artistic methodology infused in chemistry: cyanotypes' aesthetic effect comes down to chemicals (ammonium iron [III] citrate and potassium ferricyanide). A film artist with a media archaeological bent knows the amount of combination needed in testing and experimenting with chemicals or materials." (Parikka, 2015, 55). What Parikka draws attention to with the phrase *getting their hands dirty* is not simply a metaphor for material engagement. It is a description of a practice in which the artist enters the same chemical history that the emulsion already carries. The gelatin is animal collagen. The silver halides are mineral compounds. The dye couplers of a reversal emulsion are organic chemicals synthesised from petrochemical precursors. All of these materials carry histories that precede the photograph and that the photograph does not acknowledge. When the mold metabolises the gelatin, it is not merely transforming a photographic medium; it is continuing a biological process whose relationship to animal collagen long predates the invention of photography. The image that results carries this deep-material history as a visible trace, superimposed on the optical trace of the photographed scene.

Marks, approaching the analogue-digital distinction through what she calls haptic visuality, gives this material specificity a perceptual dimension that bears directly on how the transformed image addresses the viewer. She draws the distinction precisely:

> Haptic visuality is distinguished from optical visuality, which sees things from enough distance to perceive them as distinct forms in deep space: in other words, how we usually conceive of vision. Optical visuality depends on a separation between the viewing subject and the object. Haptic looking tends to move over the surface of its object rather than to plunge into illusionistic depth, not to distinguish form so much as to discern texture. It is more inclined to move than to focus, more inclined to graze than to gaze. (Marks, 2000, 162)

The phrase *graze than to gaze* does not describe a failure of vision. It describes a different relationship between the eye and the image, one in which the image refuses to yield to depth and instead presents itself as a surface to be moved across. Marks derives the term from Alois Riegl's distinction between haptic and optical images, in which the haptic image retains its character as a physical object — maintaining its status on a surface plane rather than receding into illusionistic space — while the optical image gives up its material nature in order to invite a distant view that positions the viewer as a sovereign subject. This distinction is not aesthetic preference but epistemological structure: the haptic image produces a different kind of knowledge, one that is embodied and proximate rather than distanced and cognitive. The mold-transformed frame is haptic in precisely this sense. The radial clusters, the chromatic distortions, the textures that emerge from the organism's metabolic encounter with the emulsion surface — these resist the eye's habitual move into depth and force attention onto the surface itself. The image does not open onto a space behind it; it presents a skin to be read.

This is not incidental to the fiction. The narrative of *Stains of Fear* concerns images that do not behave as images should: Jonas photographs what is in front of him and finds, in what the camera has produced, something that his intention did not place there. The film's visual register must produce this experience structurally, not just narratively. The mold provides the material means by which the image withholds its depth — turns surface, resists transparency — without ceasing to show the scene. Marks's haptic visuality names the perceptual condition this produces: the viewer moves across the image rather than into it, encountering texture where depth was expected, and this encounter is knowledge of a material history that the fiction's narrative does not explain.

What the Marks framework adds to the existing analysis of indexicality — Krauss's footprint, Bazin's mold of the world's surface — is precisely this: the mold-transformed image does not simply carry a doubled trace, it carries a doubled mode of address. The photographic layer invites optical apprehension: the viewer perceives Jonas's studio, the window frame, the building opposite, and orients within the diegetic space. The biological layer refuses this apprehension. The radial cluster of magenta and cyan across the lower quadrant of an E100 frame does not resolve into pictorial space. It presents a surface. The eye, trained by decades of optical cinema to move through the image into depth, finds itself instead moving across the image, dwelling on a texture whose origin is not the scene but the organism's encounter with the gelatin beneath the scene. This is exactly what Marks means by the haptic: a visual mode that "privileges the material presence of the image" over its "representational power" (Marks, 2000, 162). But in *Stains of Fear* this is not a stylistic choice; it is a material consequence. The haptic quality of the mold-transformed frame is the indexical record of the biological event. To see the surface is to encounter the second index. The organism makes itself visible as the limit of optical vision.

Tacita Dean, whose long advocacy for analogue film has been as theoretical as it is aesthetic, articulates the difference between analogue and digital precisely on these material grounds. For Dean, analogue implies a continuous signal, a physical flow of information between the world and the surface that receives it: it is born of the physical world and carries that origin in its material properties (Dean, cited in Knowles, 2012). Digital capture breaks this continuity. It encodes light as numerical values within a grid, quantising a continuous signal into discrete units. There is no physical surface that was chemically altered by the light from the scene; there is a file whose numerical values were determined by the sensor's response to that light. Flusser, approaching the same question from a different angle, argues that what defines the photographic image is not its material support but its status as a technical image: an image produced by an apparatus running a programme, in which the photographer operates as a functionary within the camera's predetermined possibilities (Flusser, 1983). For Flusser, the break is not between analogue and digital — both are technical images — but between images produced within the apparatus's programme and those that escape it. This distinction will become relevant when the biological transformation of the emulsion is understood as a process that occurs outside the camera's programme entirely. The mold is not a functionary of any apparatus. It does not follow a programme. Its operations exceed the category of the technical image, and this excess is the material event the film carries.

## 1.3 The Organism as a Third Material Register

Fungi do not operate on their substrates from the outside. Sheldrake's account is precise on this point: fungi grow into the materials they consume, secreting digestive enzymes that break down the substrate from within and absorbing the resulting nutrients through the hyphal walls that are simultaneously producing the digestion (Sheldrake, 2020). The organism and its substrate become, during this process, a single system undergoing shared transformation. The gelatin of a photographic emulsion, derived from animal collagen, provides the proteins and moisture that fungal metabolism requires. A film stock placed in conditions of elevated humidity and temperature does not simply attract mold from outside; it provides the conditions under which organisms already present within the gelatin layer can activate and propagate. The mold grows from within the emulsion, not on top of it.

This biological specificity has direct consequences for how the transformed images should be theorised. The standard vocabulary for describing damage to analogue film — deterioration, decay, degradation — implies an original state that has been diminished by an external force. Sheldrake's account makes this vocabulary inadequate: what happens between a fungal organism and a gelatin emulsion is not damage in this sense. It is a metabolic event in which the organism becomes part of the material it encounters. What is needed, then, is a theoretical framework adequate to the ontological claim that the boundary between organism and substrate becomes genuinely indeterminate during cultivation — not merely practically difficult to locate, but ontologically unfounded. Barad's concept of intra-action, developed in the context of quantum physics and extended to a general posthumanist ontology, offers the most precise available vocabulary for this condition.

Barad distinguishes intra-action from the more familiar term interaction. Interaction presupposes that independently determinate entities exist before they come into relation; when two things interact, each is already fully formed and the relation is secondary. Intra-action inverts this: the agencies involved — organism and emulsion, in this case — do not preexist the encounter but are constituted *through* it. Barad formulates this with precision:

> phenomena are the ontological inseparability/entanglement of intra-acting "agencies." That is, phenomena are ontologically primitive relations — relations without preexisting relata. The notion of intra-action (in contrast to the usual "interaction," which presumes the prior existence of independently determinate entities) marks an important shift in which phenomena are taken as the primary ontological unit. (Barad, 2007, 139)

The phrase *relations without preexisting relata* is the pivot. When the mold encounters the emulsion, neither the organism nor the substrate is what it was before the encounter began: the gelatin is modified by enzymatic activity, the organism's growth pattern is shaped by the substrate's chemical topology, and the resulting image is the record of both modifications simultaneously. There is no way to separate what the mold did to the emulsion from what the emulsion did to the mold, because the encounter is constitutive of both. The transformed image is thus not the product of two agents acting on each other in sequence. It is what Barad calls a phenomenon: an ontologically inseparable entanglement from which something determinate — a frame, a colour cluster, a biological pattern — emerges.

Barad's framework has been developed in the context of quantum physics and scientific practice, and there is a risk of over-extending it to a biological and photochemical situation. The extension requires care. What is being borrowed here is not the quantum mechanical structure of Barad's argument but its ontological consequence: that the relationship between organism and substrate in the cultivation process is constitutive rather than merely transformative. The mold does not act upon a pre-existing image; it co-produces, with the image's material substrate, a new material configuration. This is not a claim about the mold's intentions or awareness. It is a claim about causal structure: the outcome is underdetermined by either party alone and overdetermined by the encounter between them.

The visual evidence from the E100 cultivation bears this out with a specificity that theory alone cannot produce. The growth patterns observed on the cultivated slides do not distribute uniformly across the surface of the frame. They concentrate in the mid-tones and shadows, in the regions where silver deposits are denser — where the photochemical individuation of the emulsion, in Simondon's terms, has laid down more material for the organism to engage. The lighter areas, where the exposure produced fewer silver crystals and the gelatin is correspondingly less loaded with the residue of the photographic event, support less vigorous growth. The biological trace is literally shaped by the optical trace beneath it. The organism's metabolism is informed by the exposure decisions that produced the image: where the photographer pointed the camera, what the light source was, what the exposure latitude was set to. These photographic decisions become material conditions for the fungal encounter. The organism, in responding to them, produces a pattern that is simultaneously biological and photographic — shaped by the image without representing it, legible as growth without being legible as picture.

This mutual implication is what Barad's intra-action names with precision. The organism's growth pattern is not independent of the image; neither is the image's appearance, after cultivation, independent of the organism's metabolic logic. To describe this as the mold *transforming* the image is still to imply a sequence — first image, then organism — that the intra-action model dissolves. The encounter is simultaneous. The organism and the image become, through the cultivation process, a single phenomenon whose outcome — the magenta radial cluster, the cyan fringe where dense silver meets sparse gelatin, the preserved photographic content visible beneath and through the biological pattern — belongs to neither party exclusively. The two indices are not layers stacked in sequence. They are the emergent record of a single entangled event.

This has consequences for how the cultivation process should be described in the thesis. To say that I cultivated mold on film stock implies that the mold acted upon a stable object that I handed over to it. Barad's framework suggests instead that what occurred was a process in which the system — film stock, humidity, temperature, organism, incubation duration — co-produced an image that none of these factors could have produced independently. The artist's role in this process is, as Reble observed from his own cultivation practice, closer to that of a witness than an author. The decision was the setup: the choice of stock, the temperature, the humidity, the duration. Beyond that, the encounter was its own event.

The mold does not perceive the image in any sense continuous with human perception. But it responds to the image materially. The distribution of growth across the frame, the concentration of colonies in regions where silver deposits are denser, the chromatic variations that emerge from the specific dye architecture of the emulsion — all of these are consequences of the organism's material encounter with the photographic substrate. Something happened between the organism and the image that is neither representation nor metaphor. It is a material relation whose record is the transformed image.

Bennett's concept of the assemblage is useful here, though it must be handled with care. An assemblage, for Bennett, is a grouping of heterogeneous elements — human and nonhuman, organic and inorganic — in which no single actor is the sovereign cause of what the assemblage produces (Bennett, 2010). The mold-transformed image is the product of such a grouping: the specific chemistry of the emulsion, the metabolic logic of the organism, the temperature and humidity of the incubation environment, the duration of exposure, and the particular photographic content of the underlying image. None of these factors alone produces the result; all of them together do. The concept is analytically productive here because it identifies a specific causal structure — distributive rather than singular — not because it makes a general ontological claim about the vitality of all matter. The well-documented critiques of new materialism, particularly those of Galloway (2013) and Noys (2010), have demonstrated the risks of extending the language of agency to nonhuman entities without distinguishing between causal efficacy and intentional action. What Bennett's assemblage usefully describes, in this specific case, is that the image is produced by multiple interacting causes — a claim about causal structure, not about the mold's intentions.

What the organism introduces into the photographic image, then, is not simply disruption or damage. It is a second indexical event. The first index is photographic: the emulsion records the light reflected from the scene, producing a trace that is causally connected to what was in front of the lens. The second is biological: the emulsion records the organism's metabolic encounter with its own material substrate, producing a trace that is causally connected not to a scene but to a process. The two indices occupy the same physical surface but register different orders of event. The photographic index records an optical encounter between light and silver halide. The biological trace records a metabolic encounter between organism and gelatin. Both are material, both are causal, and both are visible in the finished image.

## 1.4 Mycoaesthetics and the Question of the Living Medium

The encounter between fungal organisms and artistic or photographic substrates is not without theoretical precedent. In the last decade, a cluster of scholars and curators have begun to develop what has been called mycoaesthetics: the study of fungal and mycological processes as constitutive dimensions of aesthetic form rather than as mere instruments or metaphors. The term has been used in overlapping but distinct ways. Derek Woods, in his 2022 essay on weak and strong mycoaesthetics, proposes a distinction between work that uses fungi as subject matter or loose symbolic resource — the weak form — and work that engages with fungal processes as materially transformative agents operating within the art object itself — the strong form. In the weak mode, the mushroom is an image: a figure of decay, symbiosis, interconnection. In the strong mode, the mycelium is an actor: its growth patterns, enzymatic activity, and temporal rhythms produce formal outcomes that cannot be predicted or controlled from outside (Woods, 2022). The distinction is not merely taxonomic; it defines different relationships between the artist's intention and the work's material outcome.

*Stains of Fear* belongs unambiguously to the strong mode in Woods's taxonomy. The mold grown on Kodak E100 does not illustrate a concept of biological transformation. It performs one. The chromatic distortions, the radial colony patterns, the differential growth responding to the silver density of the underlying image — these are not representations of biological process but its direct material inscription. The distinction is the same as the difference Reble identified in his cultivation work: the artist is not the author of these effects but the designer of the conditions under which they become possible. What the strong mode of mycoaesthetics makes available, critically, is a vocabulary that does not assimilate the organism's contribution to the artist's intention.

Cecire and Solomon's 2024 essay in *Critical Inquiry* extends this framework by situating mycoaesthetics within a broader post-anthropocentric cultural moment. They argue that the particular significance of fungi as aesthetic material lies in what they call the organism's indifference to the categories by which human culture organises living matter: fungi are neither plant nor animal, neither clearly individual nor clearly collective, neither cleanly dead nor straightforwardly alive (Cecire and Solomon, 2024). This categorical undecidability is not a deficiency but a conceptual resource. The mycelium does not fit within the taxonomic frameworks that organise Western aesthetic thought — the subject, the author, the medium, the work — and this failure to fit is precisely what makes it a useful analytical object. When a fungal organism transforms a photographic substrate, the result cannot be adequately described using any single category: it is not the photographer's work, but neither is it simply natural process. It is an encounter between human intention (the photography, the cultivation setup) and nonhuman metabolism (the organism's response to the substrate), and the result belongs to both without belonging fully to either.

Anna Tsing's account of contamination as a condition of possibility provides a further theoretical resource here (Tsing, 2015). Tsing argues, through her study of the matsutake mushroom, that the interesting events in any ecosystem — the ones that produce new forms, new relations, new possibilities — occur not in conditions of purity but in conditions of contamination: in the places where different organisms, systems, and temporalities are already mixed. The matsutake, notoriously, grows only in disturbed landscapes, in forests that have been damaged by human activity or altered by geological change. The organism flourishes in the ruins of prior systems. Tsing's argument is not nostalgic for purity; it is an argument about the generativity of mixture. Applied to the cultivation process in this thesis, contamination names the condition that makes the photographic image available to biological transformation: the emulsion's gelatin is already a kind of contamination of the mineral silver halide — an organic substrate bonded to an inorganic photosensitive compound. The mold finds in this contaminated substrate the conditions for its own elaboration.

This body of theory has developed primarily in relation to gallery installation, performance, and literary form. The work of artists such as Laura Rapp, whose mycological sculptures use fungal growth as a structural material; Saša Spačal and Mirjan Švagelj, whose installation *Myconnect* (2013) embedded living mycelium into a wearable biosensor network; and Officina Corpuscoli and their *Grow Your Own* project at the Nieuwe Instituut in Rotterdam, all engage with the living organism as a productive material force within the work. These practices share with *Stains of Fear* the commitment to fungi as a constitutive agent rather than a decorative element. But they operate in installation and performance contexts where the work's status as art is established by the gallery frame rather than by a narrative logic. The question of what happens when mycoaesthetic practice is embedded within a scripted fiction — when the fungal transformation of the image coexists with character, diegesis, and narrative causality — has not been addressed within the existing literature.

It is worth being specific about what these three fields have and have not developed, because the gap is not simply a bibliographic omission but a structural consequence of each field's founding assumptions. Mycoaesthetics, as a discourse, emerged from installation practice and from literary and ecocritical theory, both of which are comfortable with the work existing as an object of contemplation rather than a vehicle for narrative belief. The gallery installation does not ask the viewer to inhabit a diegetic world; it asks the viewer to encounter a material situation. The mold on the wall, the mycelium in the vitrine, the cultivated substrate on the pedestal — these address the viewer as things in the world, not as images of things. The fiction film does both simultaneously: it presents a world to be inhabited *and* an image to be encountered. The mycoaesthetic discourse has not had to theorise this doubling because its objects do not require it.

Vibrant materialism and new materialist film theory, meanwhile, have been developed primarily in relation to non-fictional moving-image practice. The documentary encounters the non-human — the storm, the animal, the ecosystem — as an entity already present in the world being filmed. The camera witnesses material processes rather than producing them. This is a different relationship to non-human agency than the one that obtains when the filmmaker cultivates an organism in a controlled environment and then introduces its products into a fiction. In the documentary case, the filmmaker navigates a world of pre-existing material forces; in the practice developed for *Stains of Fear*, the filmmaker designs the conditions under which a material force will operate and then incorporates its outcomes into a representational system that the force was never part of. The theoretical frameworks for the first situation are relatively developed; for the second, they are not.

Object-oriented ontology, the third relevant field, has engaged extensively with cinema — Harman's analyses of cinematic objects, Bogost's alien phenomenology applied to videogames and computational media — but has treated the apparatus as a fixed technical object rather than as a biological and material system that can itself be transformed. OOO's interest is in the withdrawal of the object: the idea that any entity exceeds its relations, that the real object cannot be fully captured by any representation or interaction. This is a productive framework for thinking about what the mold's encounter with the image *reveals* — the image's material depth, its irreducibility to its surface appearance. But OOO does not offer specific tools for thinking about the biological transformation of the apparatus's own support, because its objects tend to be stable things rather than organisms-in-process.

This is the structural gap that *Stains of Fear* occupies, and naming it precisely matters for the thesis's contribution. Three fields have developed relevant frameworks but have not converged on the problem this project poses. First, the mycoaesthetics discourse has developed primarily in installation and literary contexts, where the work does not carry the additional burden of sustaining narrative belief. Second, vibrant materialism and new materialist film theory have been applied primarily to documentary and found-footage contexts — Dolphijn and van der Tuin's work on new materialisms in film, or Alaimo's theory of trans-corporeality — where the non-human agency is encountered as something already present in the world being filmed rather than cultivated into the film's own material support (Alaimo, 2010). Third, the mycoaesthetic artists who have cultivated fungal organisms on film or photographic stock — Reble, Doing, and the broader experimental film tradition — have done so outside narrative, treating the material transformation as the subject of the work rather than as a layer within a fiction. The intersection — scripted fiction, biological transformation of the material support, sustained narrative function — is unoccupied. *Stains of Fear* is an attempt to occupy it, and to theorise the conditions under which a fiction can hold both its narrative logic and its material history simultaneously visible.

This is not merely a gap in the literature. It is a structural tension in the form itself. Fiction film operates by producing belief in a diegetic world — Metz's imaginary signifier requires the apparatus to remain invisible so that the representational surface can be inhabited. The mold-transformed image makes the apparatus visible in a specific and irreversible way: not through a self-reflexive gesture, not through a Brechtian interruption, but through the literal presence of a material event on the surface of the image. The viewer who sees the radial clusters and chromatic distortions of the E100 cannot integrate them into the diegetic world of the fiction without remainder. They exceed the fiction. And yet the fiction continues: Jonas remains in his studio, the narrative unfolds, the shots cut. What the film must hold is not a resolved tension but a productive irresolution — the biological trace and the narrative surface coexisting without either absorbing the other.

## 1.5 The Digital Frame and the Biological Trace

The structure of *Stains of Fear* involves a continuous movement between three distinct material regimes: digital capture, analogue emulsion, and biological transformation. Each regime produces a different kind of image and a different relationship between the image and its material substrate. But this movement is not symmetrical. The digital is not simply one register among three. It is the frame within which the other two are ultimately held, the technical support through which the film is projected and distributed, and its specific properties determine what survives of the analogue and biological events and what is lost.

Digital capture, as Kittler observes, operates by breaking the continuous signal of light into discrete numerical values (Kittler, 2010). The resulting file is infinitely reproducible without degradation, manipulable without material residue, and architecturally optimised for transmission and storage rather than for material persistence. This is not a deficiency; it is the specific technical character of the digital image. But it means that the digital image has no material trace in the indexical sense that Krauss describes. Its pixel values are not the residue of a chemical reaction; they are the output of a computational process. Flusser would say that the digital image completes the trajectory of the technical image: it is an apparatus-product in which the material substrate has been wholly abstracted into numerical encoding, and in which the programme that generates the image is maximally removed from any physical encounter with the world (Flusser, 1983). The image does not carry the world in its material substance; it encodes the world in numerical form.

Analogue emulsion reverses this relationship. The silver halide crystal that receives light and undergoes photochemical change is physically altered by the event it records. The resulting image is indexical in Krauss's strict sense: a physical imprint, causally connected to its referent, carrying in its material structure the trace of the specific conditions of its making (Krauss, 1977). This material specificity is also a form of vulnerability. The emulsion can be scratched, bleached, dissolved, eaten. Its transformation by the organism is, from one angle, simply an extension of this vulnerability: another material event superimposed on the photochemical event of the original exposure. From another angle, it is something more significant: a second indexical event, in which the material of the image records not the scene in front of the lens but the organism's encounter with its own substrate. The transformed emulsion carries both indices simultaneously: the optical trace of the photographed scene and the biological trace of the metabolic encounter.

This layering of indexical registers is what the movement from analogue to digital, in the final stage of the process, carries forward — and it is also what the digital frame transforms. The digital file produced by scanning the transformed analogue material encodes all of these layers simultaneously. It contains the numerical values that correspond to the optical content of the original photographic image, modified by the chromatic and textural transformations of the biological event. But the scan does not preserve the material substrate; it translates it. The gelatin is no longer present. The mold is no longer growing. The silver halide crystals are no longer physically there. What the digital file retains is the visual record of what those materials did — their effects, their traces, their colours and textures — but stripped of the physical substrate that produced them. The biological event is frozen into the digital record, but it is frozen as information, not as matter.

This translation raises a precise theoretical question that the analysis in Chapter 3 will need to track in detail. The biological trace, in its analogue form, is a material event: it occupies the same physical surface as the photographic image, and its presence can be verified by examining the emulsion under magnification. In its digital form, the biological trace is a pattern of pixel values: still visible, still causally connected to the original event through the chain of scanning and encoding, but no longer materially continuous with it. The index has been preserved as a record but detached from its substrate. Whether this constitutes a loss of indexicality or a transformation of it depends on how strictly one defines the index's requirement for physical contiguity. Krauss's account requires a causal connection; the scan preserves this, since the pixel values were determined by the physical state of the emulsion at the moment of scanning. But Krauss's account also implies a material trace — a footprint, a shadow, a death mask — and the digital file is not a trace in this physical sense. It is a numerical encoding of a trace.

What *Stains of Fear* produces, then, is an image that has passed through all three material regimes and that carries within it the residue of each passage. The digital footage of the fiction provides the narrative frame. The analogue emulsion, transformed by the organism, provides the biological trace. The digital scan re-encodes both into a file that can be projected and distributed. The resulting image is not the product of any one of these processes; it is the product of their sequence, and its specific character — the way the biological trace looks, behaves, and signifies within the digital frame — cannot be understood by analysing any single layer in isolation.

What the Arriscan produces at the end of Method 3 is not, therefore, simply a digital reproduction of a biological artefact. It is the terminus of a chain in which each translation has been indexically motivated by the previous one. The digital file is an index of the scanning lamp's response to the cultivated emulsion; the cultivated emulsion is an index of the organism's response to the printed analogue material; the printed analogue material is an index of the digital-to-analogue translation of the Alexa's sensor data; the sensor data is an index of the light arriving from the set. Remove any link and the chain is broken. What makes Method 3 theoretically distinctive is that it renders this chain's complexity as a visible texture in the final image. The viewer cannot distinguish these layers perceptually — cannot point to one cluster of pixels and say "this is from the 16mm print" and to another and say "this is from the fungal colony." But all of these layers are simultaneously present, and the image's specific visual character — its grain, its colour temperature shift, its chromatic distortions, its biological patterns — is the accumulated record of each one.

This is what distinguishes Method 3 from Methods 1 and 2 at a theoretical level, and from the simpler category of the biological index. Method 1 produces biological traces on E100 slides that are then digitally scanned and composited onto diegetic screens within the fiction: Jonas's laptop display, his camera monitor. Here, the biological transformation is presented *within* the fiction as Jonas's own photographic material, attributed diegetically to his creative practice. The mold appears, from within the narrative, to belong to Jonas — to be something he encountered in his own images. The film and the character share the same biological event from different positions: the film as material fact, Jonas as diegetic experience. Method 2 inserts the biological organism into the *production* of the image: the camera shoots through glass on which the mold grows between sessions, so that the organism intervenes in the optical path in real time. Each frame is already different because the glass substrate has changed; the image records not what the scene looks like, but what the scene looks like through an organism in the process of becoming. In all three cases, the digital frame is the eventual container. But what it contains is different in each case: a biological composite, a biologically interrupted optical record, a digitised record of a biological transformation of an analogue print of a digital original. The theoretical framework developed in this chapter must be flexible enough to hold all three.

Three production methods realise this sequence differently, and each bears a distinct theoretical weight. In Method 3 — digital footage printed to 16mm Kodak E100, cultivated, scanned back as ProRes HQ — the chain is most explicit: digital, analogue, biological, digital. The image that the viewer sees is simultaneously a digital encoding of a biological transformation of an analogue print of a digitally captured scene. Each of these translations is indexical in a different sense: the digital original records the sensor's response to light; the analogue print records the exposure lamp's response to the digital file; the biological cultivation records the organism's response to the analogue substrate; the final scan records the optical state of the cultivated emulsion. All four indexical layers are simultaneously present in the ProRes HQ file. The question that remains — and that Method 1 and Method 2 will each complicate differently — is what this layered image does when it enters a fiction: how it alters the viewer's relationship to the narrative surface, what it reveals about the conditions of its own making, and what it means for a film to carry within its digital frame the material evidence of a process that no digital apparatus could have produced.
