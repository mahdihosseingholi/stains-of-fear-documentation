# Thesis Context — Stains of Fear

*Practice-based research film. This document captures process information for use in ongoing thesis writing. Updated: April 2026.*

---

## The Film

**Title:** Stains of Fear
**Camera:** ARRI Alexa LF (digital)
**Format:** Short fiction, scripted

**Central character:** Jonas — alone in his studio at night, photographing the window of the building opposite, producing images that contain distortions he cannot explain. The distortions are not narrative invention. They are biological events: the mold that has colonised the photographic material before and after the camera recorded anything.

The biological material is never present on set as a prop or physical element. It is embedded in the logic of the script, the decoupage, and in the post-production processes that inscribe it into the image retroactively.

---

## The Three Production Methods

### Method 1 — Composited Slides

Still photographs are shot on Kodak E100 slide film. The slides are then cultivated with mold in the controlled environment (see Cultivation Setup). After cultivation, the mold-transformed slides are composited digitally in post-production onto the screens and photographs visible within the diegesis: Jonas's laptop screen, his camera display.

**What this means for the image:** The biological event appears within the story as something Jonas encounters *in his own images* — a distortion that seems to originate from what he photographed. The mold is neither a digital effect nor an intrusion from outside the fiction. It arrived on the image before the compositing, and the compositing places it within the story's logic of Jonas's self-produced images.

**Implication for thesis:** Method 1 stages the mold as a participant in the diegetic image-making chain — it inhabits the photographs that Jonas produces, not the film's own photographic layer.

---

### Method 2 — Glass/Mould Interval Camera

A camera shoots through a sealed glass surface on which mold grows continuously between sessions.

**Apparatus:**
- Two layers of simple glass sealed together to create a closed environment
- Kodak E100 stock adhered to the glass from the base side, so the emulsion is exposed to the interior
- Mounted vertically — prevents humidity from forming drops on the image surface and avoids other degradation effects
- A light source placed from behind the glass sandwich
- Camera: Nikon Z7 II
- One continuous shooting session: one frame every 30 minutes over 5–6 weeks

**What this means for the image:** The biological accumulation progressively intervenes between the camera and the scene. The mold does not transform an existing photograph — it grows *into the optical path* in real time. Each frame is physically different from the last because the glass substrate has changed.

**Implication for thesis:** Method 2 makes the mold a structural element of image formation rather than a post-production inscription. The camera does not record what the scene looks like; it records what the scene looks like *through an organism that is in the process of becoming*.

---

### Method 3 — Digital → Analogue → Biological → Digital

Digitally shot footage (from the ARRI Alexa LF) is printed onto 16mm Kodak E100 colour reversal stock. The printed film is placed in the cultivation environment and exposed to fungal cultures over approximately two months. The mold-transformed film is then scanned back into digital using an **Arriscan**, output as **ProRes HQ**.

**The transformation chain:** digital → analogue → biological → digital

**What this means for the image:** The digital image is converted into a photochemical substrate, which the organism then transforms, which is then converted back into a digital signal. Each translation in the chain produces its own material inscription — grain structure, chemical response, biological pattern — all of which accumulate inside the frame that the viewer sees. The ProRes HQ file at the end of this process carries the history of everything it has been.

**Implication for thesis:** Method 3 forces the question of what the digital image *is* at any given point in the chain — it destabilises the idea that the digital is the stable, authoritative record and positions the analogue and biological stages as equally real transformations of the image's substance.

## Timelapse Documentation (Not a Production Method)

A single E100 slide was photographed at one frame every 30 minutes across the full cultivation period, producing approximately **1,300 images** in total.

This is a documentation method, not one of the three production methods used in the film itself. The sequence records the process of a single photographic frame becoming progressively colonised by mold — biological time made visible as accumulated image change. It was used to understand the mold's behaviour, growth patterns, and the relationship between the organism's activity and the photochemical structure of the substrate.

## Cultivation Setup

- Films placed in a **closed box**
- Temperature: **27–30°C**
- Elevated humidity: water and tissue added inside the box
- Films separated from each other with **dividers**
- Duration: approximately **2 months** to produce visible mould growth
- Fungal species: **unknown** — the mould originates from the gelatin of the film stock itself, not from an externally introduced culture

The mold is not an invasive foreign organism. It is latent within the photographic material — activated by temperature and humidity. This has implications for how the biological transformation is theorised: the mold is not *added to* the image from outside; it emerges from within the photographic substrate itself.

## Material Testing — The Three Stocks

### Kodak E100 (selected)

E100 is a fine-grain colour reversal slide film. Colour reversal chemistry forms dye layers — cyan, magenta, yellow — during processing rather than coating them in advance. This gave the fungal activity a chromatic substrate to transform rather than merely consume.

**Observed behaviour:**
- Produced **radial clusters** in vivid magentas, reds, cyans, and greens
- These colours bore no relationship to the photographed scene — they emerged from the encounter between metabolic activity and the specific chemical structure of the reversal dyes
- The photographic image **remained legible** beneath and through the biological transformation
- Fungal colonies responded to the **exposure density** of the underlying image: silver deposits in darker regions offered a different substrate than lighter areas; growth patterns were shaped by this difference
- The mold's behaviour was thus contingent on the specific encounter between biological metabolism and photochemical structure — neither purely biological nor purely photographic

**Why selected:** E100 produced legible transformation while retaining photographic information. That combination — biological inscription without obliteration — was the condition necessary for the material to operate within a fiction rather than simply destroy it.

---

### Ilford Scala 50 (not selected)

Scala 50 is a black-and-white reversal film.

**Observed behaviour:**
- Produced **aggressive erosion**
- The organism stripped the emulsion **to the base** — consuming the gelatin rather than transforming its chemistry
- Created **bright voids** where the gelatin had been consumed entirely
- The photographic image remained readable but was punctuated by white absences

**Why not selected:** Where E100 offered transformation alongside legibility, Scala 50 offered destruction alongside legibility. The film required the mold to be a participant in the image, not an erasure of it.

---

### Kodak Ultra Max 400 (not selected)

Ultra Max 400 is a colour negative stock. Unlike colour reversal, negative chemistry applies dye couplers during manufacture rather than forming them during processing — a different chemical architecture.

**Observed behaviour:**
- Showed **moderate degradation**
- **Limited chromatic variation** — the organism metabolised the emulsion without producing the vivid chromatic specificity seen on E100

**Why not selected:** The resulting imagery lacked the visual distinction and legibility-within-transformation that the film required.

## Key Theoretical Threads (for Thesis Development)

These are directions implicit in the material and process — not yet fully theorised positions, but nodes that the thesis writing should address:

**1. The image as substrate, not record**
The mold treats the photographic image as a chemical environment to metabolise. The image is not destroyed — it is *inhabited*. This reframes the photograph as a material thing that exists in time, not a fixed record that transcends it.

**2. Biological contingency vs. photographic intentionality**
The mold's growth patterns are shaped by the image's exposure density — meaning the organism responds to something the photographer decided (where to point the camera, how to expose the frame). But the organism's response is not controlled or predicted. The resulting image is the product of two agencies that do not communicate: photographic intention and biological metabolism.

**3. The legibility problem**
E100 was selected precisely because it retained photographic information under biological transformation. This raises the question of what legibility is for in the context of a horror fiction. The mold must be *seen as something*: a distortion that is still recognisably linked to what was photographed. Total obliteration would be noise; total preservation would be invisibility. The mold operates in the space between.

**4. Ontological layering in Method 3**
The digital → analogue → biological → digital chain produces an image that simultaneously carries the grain of film, the chemistry of reversal processing, the biological pattern of fungal growth, and the digital interpolation of the Arriscan. Each layer is indexical — each is a real material trace. The thesis can ask what it means for the viewer to see all of these at once, without knowing the chain that produced the image.

**5. Mold as emergence from within**
The fungal species is not external — it originates from within the gelatin of the film stock. The substrate carries its own transformation latent within it, activated by environmental conditions. This is not contamination from outside; it is a capacity inherent to the material that is released. The film exploits this not as metaphor but as literal fact.

**6. The diegetic split in Method 1**
In Method 1, the mold inhabits Jonas's photographs — the images he produces within the fiction — not the film's own photographic layer. This creates a structure where the biological event is diegetically attributed to Jonas while remaining materially real in the film's actual photographic material. The fiction and the process share the same event from different positions.
